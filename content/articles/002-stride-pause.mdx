*Stride wasn't failing. That is the part I still have to explain to people, because the first assumption when you hear "I paused the startup" is that something went wrong. Something did — but not the startup.*

---

## What was happening

We had traction. Not hockey-stick traction, but the kind that makes you think you are on the right track: consistent usage, feedback that was specific rather than polite, a clear next problem to solve. By the metrics that matter in the early innings, Stride was doing what it was supposed to be doing.

The thing that was not working was me.

I am an engineer by training and by reflex. My version of building something is to get very close to the problem and stay there — close enough that I can feel when the system is under-specified, when the architecture is making promises the implementation cannot keep, when a feature that feels right on paper will produce behavior that feels wrong in use. That kind of closeness is useful in certain phases of a startup. In the phase Stride had reached, it was not what the company needed most.

What Stride needed most was someone who could hold a sales conversation and then go build exactly what the conversation surfaced. I am capable of both those things in isolation. Doing them in sequence, daily, without the space to think between them, was a shape I could not hold.

I tried to hold it for about four months. At month four, I was noticing that I was slower on everything. Not burnout slow — I was not exhausted; I was competent and fine — but the kind of slow where you can see yourself solving problems a step behind where you used to be. The lag between noticing a design decision was wrong and having the right answer was longer than it had been. That lag is a signal I take seriously.

---

## The decision

I did not quit. I paused, which is a real distinction. A pause means there is something to come back to if the shape of the company or my relationship to it changes. It means I still believe in the problem Stride is solving, and I want to be honest with the co-founders about why I am stepping back rather than performing presence while running at half capacity.

The decision took about two weeks to make, once I named what was happening. I kept trying to find a structural fix — a role adjustment, a scope reduction, a new operating rhythm — that would let me stay at full contribution. None of the structural fixes felt honest. The problem was not the structure. The problem was that the company was entering a mode that required a different kind of person than I am at the front of it, and I was not going to become that person by managing myself more carefully.

Stopping while in motion has a cost that is easy to underestimate if you have not done it. You are not stopping from rest. You are stopping from full speed, which means the momentum keeps going in you even after the official activity stops. The first two weeks after I paused felt like a weird vibration — the habit of urgency without the object of it.

---

## What the ground looked like

The disorientation passed, and what came after it was genuinely useful.

I built the four-pour method during the pause. That sounds like a small thing, but the method took an amount of attention I had not had available for the previous year. I read about siphon coffee. I watched a thirty-minute video about the physics of coffee extraction three times, not because it would make me faster at anything, but because it was interesting and I finally had the room to let it be interesting. The Kyoto trip at the end of the pause, where I found the kissaten that became entry 003 — that happened because I was not managing a sprint cycle.

The ground looked like: the time to notice things at full resolution again.

I am not trying to romanticize the pause. I am aware that I had the privilege of being able to take one, and that privilege is not universally available. I am also aware that "I paused the startup to go think about coffee" is an easy story to laugh at. I am not writing this to convince anyone that I made the right call. I am writing it because I made a call, and this is what it actually looked like from inside it.

---

## What I carry forward

The specific shape I cannot hold is: close daily contact with customers plus close daily contact with implementation, with no buffer between. I know this about myself now in a way I did not before Stride.

The specific shape I work best in is: deep contact with a problem, space to think beside it, then return to build. The think-beside-it step is not optional for me. It is where the good ideas come from, and it cannot be scheduled.

I do not know yet whether I will return to Stride, or in what form. That question is open and I am not trying to close it prematurely. What I do know is that the pause was necessary, and that I would make the same decision again, and that the entries in this archive that came after it are better than the entries that came before.

That is a reasonable thing to know about yourself.

*A recipe is a starting point. This one took about four months to stop being a starting point.*

---

## The problem I was trying to solve

Most V60 recipes I tried produced one of two results: clarity without weight, or weight without clarity. The first felt bright and thin — good acidity, but nothing holding the cup together in the back half. The second felt round and full on the finish but muddy through the front, like the extraction had worked in the wrong order.

What I wanted was both, in sequence: the front half should reveal the fruit — clean, defined, high-clarity — and the back half should carry something. Not bitterness. Not astringency. A kind of settling weight that holds the cup as it cools.

The four-pour structure is the answer I landed on.

---

## The method

**Ratio:** 18g coffee to 300ml water. Total brew time target: 3:30–3:45.

**Grind:** medium-fine. JX-Pro at 6:3 if you are on the same grinder. The grind size matters more than any of what follows — if the grind is off by a full number either direction, the pour sequencing cannot compensate.

**Water temperature:** 93°C for the first two pours. Drop to 91°C for the third and fourth. The temperature fall is not dramatic — one degree per pour after the midpoint. But it opens the third and fourth pours to extract slightly differently, which is where the back-half weight comes from.

**Bloom:** 36ml (2× dose). 40 seconds. Gentle swirl at 25 seconds. The swirl is load-bearing — it breaks the crust before the first proper pour and prevents dry channels.

**First pour (P1):** to 120ml total. Slow center pour, spiral out. Stop before hitting the paper. This is your clarity pour — the extraction rate is highest here, and you are pulling the sugars and organics that define the cup's character.

**Second pour (P2):** to 200ml total. Let the bed drop to about a third of the slurry depth before pouring. Medium pace. This is where most of the extraction happens. The pace matters: too fast and the bed agitates unevenly; too slow and you get a drawdown gap that drops the temperature.

**Third pour (P3):** to 260ml total. Let the bed drop further — to the high water line or just below. Pour gentle and direct to center. You are adding weight here without adding turbulence.

**Fourth pour (P4):** to 300ml. Same as P3. Slow. This closes the brew. The final bed should draw down clean, with a flat surface and no pooling at the edges. If you see pooling at the edges, the grind is too fine for the rate you poured.

## The bug — agitation timing

For the first six weeks of running this method I got inconsistency I could not explain. Two brews from the same bean, same grind, same everything, would produce cups that tasted like different coffees. Not dramatically different. But the kind of different that eats at you because you cannot find it in the recipe.

The bug was the bloom swirl.

I was swirling at 30 seconds instead of 25. Five seconds sounds irrelevant. It is not. At 30 seconds, the bloom has already partially set — the swirl moves the crust but does not fully break the dry pockets that form underneath in the first twenty seconds of wetting. Those pockets become dead zones in P1. The front half of the cup loses definition because the extraction is uneven from the start.

Moving the swirl to 25 seconds — while the bed is still actively releasing CO₂ and genuinely mobile — fixed the consistency problem across the next twelve brews. I have not gone back to 30 seconds.

---

## Notes on agitation and origin

The degree of bloom agitation also interacts with origin character. Naturals from Ethiopian highland (Yirgacheffe, Guji) benefit from the more aggressive 25-second swirl — they have more CO₂ to release and the early break prevents pocketing. Washed central Americans (Guatemala, Colombia light roast) can tolerate a slightly later swirl — 27–28 seconds — because the bed is more even and the agitation risk of over-turbulence is higher.

If you are brewing a delicate washed Kenyan and the cup reads bright but thin, do not add agitation — revisit the grind. Adding turbulence to a Kenyan that needs grind adjustment compounds the problem.

---

## On repeatability

The goal is not to produce the same cup every morning. Coffee changes week to week as the roast ages, and the right response to that is adjustment, not rote repetition. The goal is to have a system fine-grained enough that when a cup is off, you know which variable to move.

This method gives me that. Four clear moments of intervention; one timing critical point in the bloom; two temperature steps. When something is wrong, I can find it.

That, more than the extraction curve itself, is what the four pours are actually for.

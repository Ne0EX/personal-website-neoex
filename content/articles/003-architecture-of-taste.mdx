*In Kyoto last spring I sat in a kissaten where the master had been pulling siphon coffee for thirty-eight years. He moved like someone who had stopped trying to be impressive a long time ago. The room was hushed, the cups were warm before the coffee arrived, the chair fit my back without trying. None of it was decorated. All of it was tuned.*

That distinction — decorated versus tuned — is what this entry is trying to think through.

---

## Decoration and tuning are not the same thing

Decoration is added. Tuning is removed. Decoration asks: what can I put here that will make this better? Tuning asks: what is the thing already trying to be, and what is in its way?

The kissaten master in Kyoto had a room that had been tuned over decades. There were things in it — the siphon apparatus, the cups, the chairs, the counter — but nothing was there to impress. Everything was there because its absence would make the room less itself. That is a different criterion from aesthetic. Aesthetic is about what you add; tuning is about knowing when to stop removing.

I became aware, in that room, that most of the things I owned were decorated and most of the things I actually used were tuned. My Fujifilm X-E5 is tuned — every decision in that camera is in service of a specific use. My apartment, at the time, was decorated. Comfortable, pleasant, full of objects I had selected carefully. None of it had the quality of that room, which was: the room already knew what it was for, and it was only that.

---

## Taste as architecture

The question I came back to from that trip was not about objects. It was about the process that produces the distinction.

What is taste, exactly?

The common answer is that taste is preference. You like what you like; someone else likes something different; both are valid. This is true as far as it goes, but it does not explain the thing the kissaten master had, which was not preference but something more structural. He did not like the room in the way you might like a film or a meal. The room was *built* from a set of accumulated decisions, each one refining the previous. The taste was load-bearing. Without it, the room could not be what it was.

This is what I mean by architecture.

Taste-as-preference is episodic. Taste-as-architecture is cumulative. The architectural version is not a feeling about any individual object; it is a structural principle that you apply across decisions, over time, and the result is coherent in a way that no single choice could produce. The kissaten master's room was coherent not because every object was beautiful but because every object was there for the same reason.

---

## How it develops

What you choose to keep is the primary material. Not what you buy — what you keep. Buying is easy and produces a lot of noise. The test of taste is the selection pressure you apply when the option is to keep something versus let it go.

The thing I have noticed about my own selection pressure, reviewing it over the past few years: I keep things that reward repeated use. The V60 method. The camera. The Cormac McCarthy novels I have read three times. The essays I return to when I am thinking about a problem. The films that do not explain themselves.

I let go of things that perform — things that communicate an identity claim rather than doing a job. The job does not have to be functional. An essay can do the job of clarifying something I had previously misunderstood. A photograph can do the job of preserving the specific quality of light on a particular morning. The job is not the same as utility. It is closer to: does this thing do something, or does it merely signify doing something?

---

## The residue

What you accumulate, over years of keeping and releasing, is a residue. Not a style — that is too coherent a word for what it actually is. A residue: the aggregate impression of thousands of small decisions, most of which you have forgotten making.

The residue is what people encounter when they encounter your work, your space, your conversation. It is the product of taste-as-architecture rather than taste-as-preference. It cannot be assembled directly; you cannot decide to have it and then build it. It can only develop through the accumulation of decisions that each take it seriously.

The kissaten master did not design the room. He curated it, over time, with a criterion he probably could not fully articulate but applied consistently. The room is the residue of that criterion.

## Working notes

I am not done thinking about this. The entry is marked ongoing for a reason.

The part I am still trying to understand: how the architectural approach to taste interacts with the risk of rigidity. The criterion that produces a tuned room can, over time, calcify into a set of rules that prevents the room from evolving. The kissaten master's room worked because thirty-eight years of refinement had found a stable form. But there is a version of that story where the same commitment produces a room that has not changed since 1986 and now reads as anachronism rather than precision.

The distinction between stable and calcified is something I do not yet have a clear view of. I will come back to this entry when I do.
